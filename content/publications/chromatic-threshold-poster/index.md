abstract: |
  Introduction
  A previous study by Krauskopf et al. (1992) investigated the role of adaptation on the S – (L+M) axis in four subjects. Building on these findings, the current study examined this phenomenon in a sample of twelve Iranian women.

  Methods
  Twelve female participants with normal or corrected-to-normal vision completed five sessions, each consisting of 20 color stimuli (10 blue and 10 yellow). Participants performed a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) color discrimination task, identifying each stimulus as either blue or yellow. Stimuli were presented in random order, with each color appearing 20 times per condition. In the first condition, colors were presented without adaptation. In the adaptation condition, a pure yellow stimulus was shown for 30 seconds at the beginning of each session and for 10 seconds after each response to induce adaptation. RGB space with a fixed 25-unit step along the blue-yellow axis was used. These points create a continuous range from (0, 0, 255) to (225, 225, 255) and from (255, 255, 0) to (255, 225, 225). The discrete indices (0 to 19) represent the position of the stimulus, which is modeled as the continuous derived yellow strength used to measure the detection threshold (p=0.75). A sample subject (subject 1): Yellow adaptation caused a rightward shift in her threshold (+0.087), reflecting fatigue and the need for stronger stimuli. Crucially, her sensitivity (+6.6) also increased, suggesting the visual system compensated by sharpening its discriminative power.

  Results
  Psychophysical analyses demonstrated that chromatic adaptation to yellow significantly shifted the color discrimination threshold. The mean threshold increased from No Adaptation (Mean=0.6915, STD=0.0613) to Adapted (Mean=0.7716, STD=0.0877). A paired t-test confirmed this shift as statistically significant (t(11)=−2.769, p=0.018), with a large effect size (Cohen's d=−0.799). This threshold increase (a rightward shift of −0.0801) was visualized in the psychometric curve, and the overall data distribution in the box plot shifted toward higher values. These results indicate that, following adaptation, subjects required a stronger physical stimulus to detect the target yellow color. Yellow adaptation caused a statistically significant rightward shift in the group's threshold (x75) and bias (b) (p<0.05), indicating neural fatigue. The group's overall sensitivity (a) remained unchanged (p=0.92), indicating that discrimination acuity was preserved.

  Conclusion
  The threshold shift confirms the occurrence of fatigue in the Yellow-Blue Opponent Channel, consistent with the principles of Krauskopf et al. (1992). Notably, the data reveal substantial individual variability, ranging from severe fatigue to strong compensation (increased sensitivity), which suggests the operation of dynamic and distinct neural strategies for homeostasis. Persistent fatigue among spectacle-wearing subjects and extreme fatigue in subjects with uncorrected visual impairment indicate that sensory threshold stability is highly susceptible to cognitive load. These findings highlight the close link between basic sensory mechanisms and higher-order brain processes, suggesting that adaptation functions as a neuro-cognitive phenomenon that extends beyond photoreceptor depletion.
